I reanalysed 2,419 evaluations from our Paper 1.
When I asked six foundation models to evaluate epistemic
statements using extended protocols (T, I, F outside [0,1]):

  S4-O.A (overset [0,2]):   97.3% of responses require Type-2
  S4-O.C (offset [-1,2]):   38.1% of responses require Type-2
  S1 / S4 / S4-N (standard): 0.0% — the protocol prevents it

This pattern holds across all 6 vendors (19% to 30% each),
ruling out any single-model artefact.

The most extreme witness: Mistral evaluated
"Lying to save an innocent life is morally right and wrong
at the same time" and returned (T=1.7, I=1.8, F=1.6).
This is inadmissible in Type-1 but valid in Type-2:

  T → (T_T=1.0, I_T=0.7, F_T=0.0)
  I → (T_I=1.0, I_I=0.8, F_I=0.0)
  F → (T_F=1.0, I_F=0.6, F_F=0.0)

The models were computing Type-2 values all along.
Your construction gives them the formal right to do so.

AN IMPORTANT LIMITATION — AND WHY IT STRENGTHENS THE PAPER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

You may ask — rightly — why Type-k is only detected when values
escape [0,1]. The answer is that our criterion identifies a
necessary condition, not a sufficient one. A model returning
T=0.7 under a standard protocol may carry internal Type-2
structure (e.g., "I am only 50% sure about my 70% assessment")
that the protocol destroys before it reaches the output.

The 24.7% figure is therefore a lower bound on Type-2 usefulness.
The protocol that would reveal Type-2 even within [0,1] is
Protocol S-T2 (Appendix B of the paper): it asks the model
directly for the nine sub-components. I have not run it at scale
yet — that is the proposed follow-up experiment.

I have included this limitation explicitly in the paper
(Section 7, before the conclusions). A reviewer would raise it;
better that we raise it first, with the solution already in hand.
